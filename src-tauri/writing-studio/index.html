There is a particular kind of quiet that exists only before the rest of the world wakes up. The streets are still, the inbox is silent, and for a brief while, time feels like it belongs entirely to you.

I used to think that productive mornings meant getting as much done as possible before 9 a.m. I would wake up, immediately reach for my phone, and begin answering emails before I'd even left bed. It felt efficient. It also left me exhausted before the day had properly begun.

Over the past year, I've been experimenting with something different: doing less in the morning, but doing it with more attention.

Attention is a finite resource

We tend to treat attention as though it renews endlessly throughout the day. It doesn't. Every notification we check, every decision we make, and every context switch takes something from a limited reserve.

When we spend that reserve reactively—responding to whatever is loudest or most urgent—we give away the best part of our thinking before we've decided what deserves it.

A slower morning isn't about laziness. It's about being deliberate with the first and often clearest hours of the day.
